Research

My research focuses on the intersection of monetary economics and expectations that deviate from the standard Full Information Rational Expectations (FIRE) hypothesis. This agenda is motivated by the large body of empirical research challenging FIRE, and my contribution has been to build models that reconcile theory with this evidence.

Job Market Paper

Target-Based Communication under Credibility-Distorted Beliefs

Job market paper, October 2026

Abstract. Angeletos and Sastry (2021) theoretically show that central bank (CB) target-based communication anchors inflation expectations better than its interest rate counterpart. However, more UK households report adjusting their expectations based on the interest rate, they under-react to new information, and experienced inflation lowers credibility among the cohorts that have lived through the strongest inflationary episodes. This raises the question of whether intensively investing in target-based communication would anchor expectations and deliver welfare improvements in this context. I explore it by proposing an expectation mechanism that unifies experience-based expectations with endogenous credibility, distorted by the diagnostic expectations of Bordalo, Gennaioli and Shleifer (2018), and incorporate it into a Representative Agent (RANK) and a Heterogeneous Agent (HANK) New Keynesian model. The main findings are: (i) while in the RANK model the welfare loss is entirely due to distorted expectations, in the HANK model the burden shifts from the older cohorts, who remember differently, towards the younger cohorts, who spend differently; (ii) a policy fully focused on communicating the target is unequivocally superior in reducing the loss for all cohorts; and (iii) target communication and traditional monetary policy are complements in the RANK model and substitutes in the HANK model. Therefore, target-based communication is a highly effective tool to anchor expectations when credibility is eroded, which CBs could implement more intensively with ample welfare gains.

Sticky Information Meets Wealth in Utility: Implications for Monetary Policy

Working paper, April 2025

I build a New Keynesian model that combines two frictions: agents update information infrequently, and they derive utility from wealth. Then, I analyze how this combination may change standard results in monetary economics for two types of monetary rules: a Taylor rule and a Wicksellian rule. The paper demonstrates that equilibrium determinacy can be achieved under a Taylor rule even with a passive monetary policy, while price-level rules require both information frictions and a small inflation response. The transmission of monetary policy is modified, since agents’ preference for wealth and information frictions extend the effects of shocks, requiring longer periods of low interest rates. Welfare analysis shows that inflation targeting becomes more desirable than price-level targeting when wealth enters utility, even when price-level targeting performs better in the standard New Keynesian model. Finally, optimal policy rules remain robust, but wealth in the utility function would shift the central bank’s preference towards output stabilization.

Unanchoring Regimes under Social Learning

I study why inflation expectations become unanchored quickly but re-anchor slowly, and what this asymmetry implies for monetary policy. Using microdata from the Bank of Mexico’s Survey of Professional Forecasters, I build a New Keynesian model in which forecasters form social learning expectations, as in Grimaud, Salle and Vermandel (2025) and Bullard, Grimaud, Salle and Vermandel (2026). While other deviations from FIRE, such as cognitive discounting and diagnostic expectations, fail to generate any unanchoring episode, social learning generates them with the frequency and persistence seen in the data. The bottom line is a Brainard (1967)-type prescription for caution in monetary policy.

Monetary Policy Surprises, Central Bank Information, and the Distribution of Inflation Expectations: Evidence from Mexico

I identify monetary policy and central bank information shocks from high-frequency movements in interest rate futures around 185 Bank of Mexico announcements between 2005 and 2026, following Jarociński and Karadi (2020), and analyze their effects on the full distribution of professional forecasters’ inflation expectations.

Optimal Simple Monetary Policy Rules and Equilibrium Determinacy under Informality

M.A. thesis, El Colegio de México, 2016 · Josué Sáenz Award for Best Master’s Thesis

I build a two-sector New Keynesian model with formal labor market frictions to study how informality shapes the design of monetary policy. The informal sector buffers the formal economy against shocks, so a simple Taylor rule that responds only to formal inflation and output delivers welfare gains without requiring the central bank to measure the informal sector. However, informality narrows the determinacy region, so economies with large informal sectors must respond to inflation more aggressively to avoid welfare-reducing multiple equilibria.

Is a Dual Mandate Optimal under Informality and Capacity Constraints?

I extend the New Keynesian model with informality and an occasionally binding capacity constraint on formal employment, capturing the idea that the informal sector absorbs the workers the formal sector cannot hire, and study whether adding output stabilization to the central bank’s mandate is justified. Preliminary results show that a dual mandate delivers less stabilization and costs more inflation in an economy with a large informal sector.

State-Dependent Pricing, Firm Heterogeneity, and the Slope Puzzle

I ask why the Phillips curve is nearly flat in normal times but steeper during shocks. Using the micro price quotes underlying the UK consumer price index from 1996 to 2025, a dataset to which I contribute (ukmicrocpi.com), I document that large national retail chains adjust prices almost four times as often as independent local retailers and account for the vast majority of the steepening of the slope during crises. A menu-cost model in which firms differ in their exposure to aggregate shocks is proposed to replicate this behavior.
