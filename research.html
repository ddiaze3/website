Job Market Paper

Target-Based Communication under Credibility-Distorted Beliefs

Job market paper, October 2026

Abstract. Angeletos and Sastry (2021) theoretically show that central bank (CB) target-based communication anchors inflation expectations better than its interest rate counterpart. However, more UK households report adjusting their expectations based on the interest rate, they under-react to new information, and experienced inflation lowers credibility among the cohorts that have lived through the strongest inflationary episodes. This raises the question of whether intensively investing in target-based communication would anchor expectations and deliver welfare improvements in this context. I explore it by proposing an expectation mechanism that unifies experience-based expectations with endogenous credibility, distorted by the diagnostic expectations of Bordalo, Gennaioli and Shleifer (2018), and incorporate it into a Representative Agent (RANK) and a Heterogeneous Agent (HANK) New Keynesian model. The main findings are: (i) while in the RANK model the welfare loss is entirely due to distorted expectations, in the HANK model the burden shifts from the older cohorts, who remember differently, towards the younger cohorts, who spend differently; (ii) a policy fully focused on communicating the target is unequivocally superior in reducing the loss for all cohorts; and (iii) target communication and traditional monetary policy are complements in the RANK model and substitutes in the HANK model. Therefore, target-based communication is a highly effective tool to anchor expectations when credibility is eroded, which CBs could implement more intensively with ample welfare gains.

Working Papers

Unanchoring Regimes under Social Learning

Working paper, 2026

Abstract. Why do inflation expectations become unanchored quickly but re-anchor slowly, and what does this asymmetry imply for monetary policy? Using microdata from the Bank of Mexico’s Survey of Professional Forecasters, I build a New Keynesian model in which forecasters form their beliefs through social learning. While other deviations from full-information rational expectations, such as cognitive discounting and diagnostic expectations, fail to generate any unanchoring episode, social learning generates them with the frequency and persistence seen in the data. Since the central bank cannot separate fundamental inflation from expectation drift, responding to inflation more aggressively than a standard Taylor coefficient reduces the share of time spent unanchored but raises output volatility by more than it gains in anchoring, a Brainard-type prescription for caution.

Sticky Information Meets Wealth in Utility: Implications for Monetary Policy

Working paper, April 2025

Abstract. This paper extends the New Keynesian model by incorporating Sticky Information and Wealth in Utility (the SIWUNK model), to address its implications for the design of monetary policy. This paper demonstrates that equilibrium determinacy can be achieved under a Taylor rule even with a passive monetary stance, while price-level rules require both information frictions and a discrete inflation response. The transmission of monetary policy is modified, since agents’ preference for wealth and delayed information processing extend the effects of shocks, requiring prolonged periods of low interest rates. Welfare analysis reveals that inflation targeting becomes more desirable than price-level targeting when wealth enters utility, even when price-level targeting performs better in the New Keynesian model. Finally, despite the information frictions, optimal policy rules remain robust, though wealth in the utility function could shift the central bank preference towards output stabilization.

Work in Progress

Monetary Policy Surprises, Central Bank Information, and the Distribution of Inflation Expectations: Evidence from Mexico

I identify monetary policy and central bank information shocks from high-frequency movements in interest rate futures around 185 Bank of Mexico announcements between 2005 and 2026, and study their effects on the full distribution of professional forecasters’ inflation expectations.

State-Dependent Pricing, Firm Heterogeneity, and the Slope Puzzle

Why is the Phillips curve nearly flat in normal times but steeper during shocks? Using the micro price quotes underlying the UK consumer price index from 1996 to 2025, I document that large national retail chains are under four percent of firms but roughly seventy percent of the consumption basket, adjust prices almost four times as often as independent local retailers, and account for most of the increase in the slope during crises. A menu-cost model in which firms differ in their exposure to aggregate shocks is intended to replicate this behavior.

Is a Dual Mandate Optimal under Informality and Capacity Constraints?

I extend the New Keynesian model with an informal sector and an occasionally binding capacity constraint on formal employment, and ask whether adding output stabilization to the central bank’s loss function helps stabilize output. Preliminary results show that a dual mandate delivers less stabilization and costs more inflation in an economy with a large informal sector.
